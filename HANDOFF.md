# Handoff

What was built, what it does, where it is thin, and what most needs a human to look at.

This is the Phase 9 document from [the work order](docs/design/14-work-order.md#phase-9--handoff).
It is written to be read once, in order, by whoever picks the project up next.

---

## The short version

Every phase in [ROADMAP.md](ROADMAP.md) is implemented. The game builds to a single
self-contained executable with `./build.sh`, runs in first person, simulates all four
loops against one clock, plays in co-op up to five players, loads mods, and validates
roughly a thousand content definitions against frozen schemas in CI.

What it does not have is **a human who has played it**. Everything below that sounds like
a balance judgement came from a bot, and a bot is a liveness probe rather than a player.
That is the single largest risk in the project and the whole reason this document exists.

## What to run first

```bash
./build.sh run        # build and play it
tools/run_tests.sh    # the whole suite
python3 tools/validate_content.py
tools/nightly_bot.sh  # the telemetry sweep, then read tools/telemetry_summary.py's output
```

`./build.sh` fetches its own pinned Godot and export templates into `.tooling/`. Nothing
needs installing first, and nothing is written outside the repository.

## Deviations from the spec, and why

- **The base game's bulk content is generated, not hand-authored.** `tools/generate_content.py`
  emits the manifest's volume from tables; the worked examples the documentation points at
  are hand-authored and protected from it by name. A thousand hand-written definitions
  would have been a thousand chances to drift from the schemas. The cost is that the
  content is broad and even rather than surprising — a content designer should expect to
  replace much of it, and the generator is structured so that they can do it a table at a
  time.
- **Art and audio are absent rather than placeholder-quality.** No sound ships at all. The
  room is primitives. Documents and cards are drawn as runtime vector art, which *is* the
  intended approach for those — everything around them is not.
- **The `case` verification family is implemented through the same engine as identity**,
  exactly as the spec asks, but the condition-grading minigame is a number the player
  commits to rather than a physical inspection of centering and corners. The hooks are
  there (`assessed_grade` versus `true_grade` drives every payoff); the interaction is not.
- **Steam is scripted but never exercised.** [`steam/`](steam/) and the release workflow are
  written to the SteamPipe documentation. Nobody has uploaded a build.

## Known issues, worst first

1. **Nobody has played it.** Every balance number below came from a bot with a deliberately
   simple policy. The bot cannot tell you whether a shift is *fun*, whether the counter is
   legible under pressure, or whether ripping a box feels like anything.
2. **Carelessness is punished, but not by much, and the margin is thin.** The design's
   central claim is that scrutiny is a budget and that spending none of it is worse than
   spending it badly. Measured over four bot days at the standard preset with three
   players, a triaging shop now ends ahead of a careless one on reputation (2.0 against
   1.0) and on progression (four licences against two) — but the careless shop is still
   ahead on cash and on net worth, and it took two fixes to get even that far:
   - Approving a transaction without using the tools you own no longer pays the
     "served well" bonus. Reputation also accrues with throughput in the kitchen and the
     library, so before this, volume simply beat judgement.
   - A scheduled suspicious encounter whose document had no vector the player's toolkit
     could catch used to be sent as an honest customer with the slot spent anyway. Three
     of twenty-eight document types have no naked-eye vector at all, so this was a real
     hole. The generator now falls back to a gross vector and the director takes the slot
     back unspent.

   What is left is a question only a human can answer: three suspicious encounters a day
   against roughly ninety transactions is a 3% rate, and each miss costs a reputation
   point against the 0.012 an ordinary good transaction earns. That arithmetic works, but
   it makes the counter rare rather than central. Raising `suspicious_per_shift`, or
   scaling it with counter traffic, is a one-line experiment — but which way it should go
   is a question about how the game feels.

   A caution on how any of this is measured: comparing two bot policies' net worth
   cannot settle a question about one mechanic, because two policies differ in what they
   serve, cook, clean, audit and buy. The claim that opening sealed stock loses money is
   asserted against the sealed ledger itself, which compares like with like; the claim
   that judgement beats volume is asserted against triage, which differs from
   carelessness in exactly that.

   Two more things the bot revealed and nobody should rely on. The careless policy is
   partly saved from itself by poverty: it declines the high-value trade-ins it cannot
   fund, and those are exactly where the forgeries concentrate. And the *exhaustive*
   policy — reach for every tool on every customer — ends with worse reputation than the
   careless one (-0.3 against 4.4 over four days), because it serves a tenth as many
   people. That is what the design asks for, so no test asserts otherwise, but it is
   worth knowing that the punishment for over-checking is currently much larger than the
   punishment for not checking at all.

3. **Solo play trails three-player on reputation.** Licence pace is inside the design's 15%
   tolerance across player counts, which is the gate. Reputation is not: a lone player
   eats more walk-outs than the pacing compensates for.
4. **The kitchen and the counter compete more than they interleave.** In bot runs the
   kitchen is either starved or dominant depending on policy, rather than trading off
   moment to moment. Suspected cause is that cooking occupies a slot for a fixed span
   instead of being interruptible.
5. **Sealed product's tail may be too flat.** A serialised card turns up in roughly one box
   in nine and covers a little over the box's price. That is defensible, and it is the kind
   of number that should be set by how it feels rather than by arithmetic.
6. **The opening shop is not thin, and the spec says it should be.** `open_for_business`
   stocks a dozen units of every product in the catalogue, free. That is roughly thirty
   thousand of inventory before the first customer, which softens the opening week the
   design wants tight and makes net-worth comparisons between policies noisy, because
   stock dominates the number. It is a one-line change and every balance figure above
   would move with it, which is why it has been left for a deliberate pass rather than
   folded in quietly.
7. **No save-compatibility corpus.** Migrations are implemented and tested, but there are no
   saves from older versions to migrate, because there have been no older versions.

## What the verification layers say

All five layers from [13 Testing](docs/design/13-testing-and-verification.md) run in CI:

- **Static** — ruff, gdlint, gdformat, yamllint, markdownlint, codespell, pinned in
  `.pre-commit-config.yaml`.
- **Schema and content** — every definition against its schema, plus the design's own
  quality gates: every forgery catchable with a tool the player can have, every licence
  unlocking something, every product reachable, and sealed product that never pays better
  opened than sold.
- **Unit and integration** — the gdUnit4 suite, run headless.
- **Simulation** — the bot sweep across presets, player counts and seeds.
- **Visual** — in-game screenshot capture compared against approved baselines.

The licence audit asserts that every asset carries a machine-readable record and
regenerates [CREDITS.md](CREDITS.md) from them.

## Where the bodies are buried

Things that will bite someone who does not know them:

- **GDScript lambdas capture locals by value.** A tally accumulated in a captured `int`
  silently stays zero. The test suite uses Dictionary and Array accumulators for this
  reason, and says so where it does.
- **`StringName` arrays do not sort lexicographically.** Anywhere order must be stable
  (mod load order, event dispatch), sort through `String`.
- **The director is consulted at the counter, not at the door.** Asking earlier spends the
  shift's budget of suspicious encounters on people who only wanted a table.
- **Reputation is asymmetric on purpose.** It is built by ordinary competence a little at a
  time and lost in single expensive moments. Any change that makes a mistake cheap removes
  the reason to check anything.
- **`content/` is a mod.** If something works for base content and not for a mod, the bug is
  in the privilege, not in the mod.

## What to do next, in order

1. **Put it in front of players.** Ten minutes each, no instructions, and watch where they
   look. The onboarding claim — that the world teaches the verbs — is untested.
2. **Tune the scrutiny budget** against issue 2 above, with the bot sweep as the regression
   net rather than the source of truth.
3. **Audio.** Every cue already has a visual counterpart, so the wiring is done and the
   work is asset-shaped.
4. **An art pass on the room.** The documents are already right; the shop is not.
5. **A content designer on the generated bulk**, one table at a time.
